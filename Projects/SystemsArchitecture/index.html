Systems Architecture for General Aviation Aircraft System

  • 184,212 feasible architectures
  • 4 evaluation metrics
  • Tradespace & Pareto analysis
  • NSGA-II down-selection

This project develops a systems architecture for a next-generation family of general aviation aircraft. The work applies systems architecture philosophies from stakeholder and goal analysis through functional, operational, and physical architecture, to enumerate and evaluate hybrid-electric design alternatives, visualize multi-objective tradespace, and down-select Pareto-efficient architectures using a genetic algorithm. The work compares how propulsion, energy storage, avionics, redundancy, and subsystem integration choices affect operating cost, certification schedule, reliability, and durability across more than 184,000 feasible architectures, supporting evidence-based architecture decisions before detailed design.

Systems engineering

Architecture decisions framed as an explicit design space, evaluated against program objectives, and compared through tradespace analysis so architecture selection is evidence-based rather than ad hoc.

Stakeholder analysis

The study framed a resilience-first family of next-generation general aviation aircraft as a socio-technical system—led by a government innovation body and a Part 23–experienced manufacturer—before any architecture enumeration.

Project context

The study targets a modular 4–8 seat GA platform that reduces single-point failure risk, improves survivability, and lowers lifecycle carbon impact through hybrid-electric integration—at conceptual architecture level, not detailed aerodynamics or structural sizing. Reference systems include conventional piston aircraft (Cessna 172 class), Cirrus SR-series with CAPS, and distributed-electric demonstrators such as NASA X-57.

Stakeholder value network

Value flows were modeled as a hub-and-spoke network with rim connections: the project/system sits at the center; radial exchanges (e.g., certification data with FAA, safety statistics with insurers) combine with lateral flows (owners ↔ insurers, passengers ↔ owners, schools ↔ MROs). Each flow received semi-quantitative importance and benefit scores (I×B) to rank stakeholder influence.

Most important stakeholders (weighted stakeholder occurrence on cumulative flow scores):

  1. Aircraft owners (62) — financial nexus linking capital, infrastructure, and demand
  2. Passengers (36) — ultimate revenue and safety/comfort drivers
  3. Flight schools & investors (33) — fleet demand and capital intensity

The method can under-rank operational roles (pilots) and boolean gatekeepers (FAA certification) relative to transaction volume—limitations addressed qualitatively in later goal prioritization.

Needs → system goals

Stakeholder needs were translated into twenty solution-neutral goals (G1–G20) with Must / Should / Might priorities and traceability to needs—forming the criteria used in functional architecture, enumeration constraints, and evaluation metrics.

Functional and physical architecture

This phase defines what the system must do (functional decomposition, goal traceability, operational states) and how it is physically realized (subsystem hierarchy, interfaces, function-to-component allocation, and connectivity).

Functional architecture

Six Level-1 functions (F1–F6) decompose the next-generation GA aircraft: generate and manage propulsion, control flight path, monitor and ensure safety, manage avionics and communications, support cabin and payload, and enable maintenance and health management. Each branches into Level-2 sub-functions with explicit data and control flows.

Operational architecture: Two SysML views define how the system behaves in operation—a lifecycle state machine for ground-to-air phases and emergency paths, and a primary use-case activity diagram for the passenger transport mission.

The activity diagram represents the primary use case where passengers are transported safely to their destination. Fuel enables generate propulsion, producing thrust for control flight path, which governs aircraft movement. The flight plan is processed by manage avionics and monitored by monitor safety to ensure safe navigation. In parallel, passengers are supported through support cabin and supervised by health management to maintain acceptable cabin and system conditions. All functions collectively enable controlled flight, resulting in passengers arriving safely at the destination.

Physical architecture

Five physical subsystems structure the implementation: propulsion, electric and energy, flight and control, safety and support, and airframe. Level-1 physical BDD and IBD define component hierarchy and cross-subsystem interfaces (power, control signals, sensor feedback, fault alerts). Level-2 allocated architecture maps 22 sub-functions onto physical components; a design structure matrix (DSM) captures mechanical, electrical, informational, and fluid coupling across 19 components.

Physical decomposition and DSM connectivity inform later LRU partitioning (D7) and integration constraints used in architecture enumeration.

Enumeration

Eight architecture decisions (D1–D8) are encoded with canonical types (standard form, down-select, partition, binary). Full factorial expansion and constraint propagation yield every feasible hybrid-electric architecture—no sampling.

184,212 Feasible architectures
324 Independent backbone (D1×D2×D3×D5×D6×D8)
8 Design decisions
4 ADG constraint rules

Enumeration in R (archr) proceeds in stages:

  • Standard-form decisions — D1 propulsion topology, D2 battery chemistry, D3 motor count, D5 FCC redundancy, D6 avionics integration, D8 CAPS
  • Down-select (D4) — six binary motor installation sites (wing root/tip, nose, tail), coupled to D1 and D3
  • Partition (D7) — six functional subsystems grouped into k = 2 or 3 LRU packages, constrained by D5
  • Constraint filtering — propulsion and motor-count rules; FCC redundancy vs feasible partitions

The architectural decision graph (ADG) documents dependencies so infeasible combinations are pruned before evaluation—reducing a much larger raw combinatorial space to 184,212 valid designs.

Evaluation

Each of the 184,212 feasible architectures maps to four system-level metrics using deterministic value functions grounded in stakeholder goals. The full evaluated set is then explored through tradespace visualization, Pareto ranking, sensitivity analysis, and feature mining to identify dominant design choices.

Evaluation metrics

Operating cost ($/hr)

Additive subsystem costs. Traces to G6, G10 — operators and airlines need predictable hourly economics.

Certification schedule (years)

Hybrid series–parallel critical path across propulsion, control, and avionics. Traces to G2, G20 — regulators and program managers need feasible certification timelines.

Propulsion reliability

Series–parallel reliability block diagram. Traces to G1, G14, G18 — safety-critical redundancy and fault tolerance.

System durability (years)

Min–max and redundancy-aware lifetimes. Traces to G4, G8 — maintenance teams and owners need component longevity.

Aggregation uses additive cost where subsystems are separable, critical-path certification for sequential dependencies, series–parallel reliability for coupled failure chains, and min–max durability for weakest-link and redundancy effects.

Tradespace analysis

Pairwise and multi-metric views of the full feasible set (no subsampling): operating cost, certification schedule, propulsion reliability, and system durability.

Tradespace findings

The tradespace is not a smooth continuous surface—it shows discrete structure driven by architectural decisions rather than fine tuning within a single configuration.

  • Reliability stratification: Cost vs. reliability exhibits horizontal bands—propulsion reliability takes discrete tiers driven mainly by propulsion topology (D1) and motor configuration (D3/D4). Within a band, cost varies widely, so moving to a higher reliability tier requires changing discrete choices, not incremental adjustment.
  • Certification clustering: Cost vs. certification forms grid-like bands dominated by FCC redundancy (D5), avionics integration (D6), and LRU partitioning (D7). Cost spreads within each schedule band, with a weak tendency for higher-cost designs to sit in longer-certification bands.
  • Multi-objective tension: Parallel coordinates show bundling and equivalence classes—many configurations share identical metrics—alongside tradeoffs between cost/schedule and reliability/durability, with some lines remaining balanced across all four objectives near the Pareto region.

Fuzzy Pareto frontier

Architectures were sorted by Pareto rank across all four objectives. A fuzzy frontier (rank < 10) highlights the near-optimal region in two-dimensional views while the true rank-one front captures non-dominated designs.

Rank-one designs share recurring traits: series hybrid topology (D1 = 0), at least one wing motor but not more than two across wing stations, no nose or tail motors (D4), ballistic parachute installed (D8 = 1), and predominantly three LRU partitions (D7)—though many partition assignments are metrically equivalent under the current model.

  • Minimal wing propulsion without nose/tail motors favors lower cost and shorter certification while trading some reliability vs. more distributed motor layouts.
  • Equivalence classes appear when different D7 assignments yield identical cost, schedule, reliability, and durability—limiting how much the Pareto set discriminates among partition schemes.

Dominant decisions

Sensitivity, connectivity, Simmons four-quadrant charts, and association-rule feature mining identify which decisions drive metrics and which combinations appear consistently in the good (fuzzy Pareto) region.

Architectural hubs (high sensitivity + high connectivity): D1 propulsion topology and D3 motor count dominate cost, reliability, and durability—they should be frozen early because changes cascade through the design. D2 battery chemistry and D5 FCC redundancy are also high-impact for specific metrics.

Independent levers (high sensitivity, low connectivity): D6 avionics integration and D8 CAPS shift certification or cost with limited coupling to propulsion layout—candidates for later trade studies.

Feature mining on the fuzzy Pareto set:

  • Series hybrid (D1 = 0) is a near-universal requirement for architectures in the good region.
  • No nose/tail motors (D4) appears in ~88% of high-performing designs—wing-restricted propulsion is strongly preferred.
  • Series hybrid + CAPS (D1 = 0, D8 = 1) shows the strongest synergistic lift toward the Pareto region.
  • Single motor (D3 = 0) is an anti-feature—reliability penalties dominate any cost savings.

Exhaustive evaluation and Pareto ranking over the full set is informative for tradespace structure, but cost grows with every additional architecture. The next step uses a genetic algorithm as a scalable alternative to obtain the Pareto front without evaluating every design.

Genetic algorithm

Enumerating and evaluating every feasible architecture is the ground truth for tradespace analysis, yet it becomes increasingly time-consuming as the decision space grows. Ranking the full set to obtain the Pareto front means running the value model on each design—feasible for this study’s 184,212 architectures, but impractical at larger scale. A multi-objective genetic algorithm is the alternative: it searches the discrete space and approximates the Pareto frontier with far fewer evaluations, making it practical to surface strong architectures for down-selection.

Why a genetic algorithm?

Full factorial enumeration plus metric evaluation scales linearly with combinatorics. Tradespace plots and fuzzy-Pareto analysis in the previous section required evaluating the entire feasible set; for higher architecture counts that workflow is slow and repetitive. NSGA-II (via rmoo in R) was used instead to explore the same decision variables and objectives efficiently—converging on a competitive Pareto region without exhaustively scoring every chromosome.

NSGA-II implementation

  • Encoding: 26-bit chromosome from 18 discrete decision elements (int2bit / bit2int)
  • Population: 200 individuals · 200 generations · uniform crossover · low-probability mutation
  • Selection: NSGA-II non-dominated sorting and crowding distance
  • Feasibility: Repair for D3–D4 coupling and ADG rules before fitness evaluation

The run converges quickly (best fitness stabilizes near iteration 20), recovering a structured Pareto region aligned with dominant decisions D1, D3, D5, and D7 from tradespace analysis—supporting use of the GA to identify competitive architectures with much less computation.

GA-selected architectures in the full tradespace

The NSGA-II run returns Pareto-optimal candidates without evaluating all 184,212 architectures. Plotting the GA down-selections against the full evaluated tradespace (red markers) shows they sit on competitive regions across every pairwise metric view—minimum cost at a target reliability band, favorable durability–cost combinations, and strong certification–schedule positions—achieved with a population–generation search rather than exhaustive ranking.

Tradespace views I — cost, reliability, and durability

Reliability stratifies into discrete horizontal tiers while cost varies widely within each tier; the GA selection lands on the cost-efficient edge of its reliability band. Durability vs. cost shows vertical bands of equivalent durability with many cost levels—the selected design pins the favorable low-cost point for its durability target.

Tradespace views II — cost, certification, and reliability

Cost vs. certification exhibits horizontal bands at discrete schedule levels; the highlighted architecture achieves competitive cost within its certification band. Certification vs. reliability shows the coupled grid structure from discrete FCC, avionics, and propulsion choices—the GA solution remains in the high-performing region without scanning every point in the cloud.
